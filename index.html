About the Garuda Purana

The Garuda Purana is one of the eighteen Mahapuranas of Hindu literature, presented as a dialogue between Lord Vishnu and Garuda, his eagle mount. It is especially renowned for its second section, which describes the soul's journey after death: the passage through Yamaloka, the reckoning before Yamaraja and his scribe Chitragupta, and the various Narakas (hells) said to correspond to particular categories of wrongdoing.

Traditionally, the text is recited during funeral rites (the "Garuda Purana Path") to guide mourners in reflecting on ethical living, duty (dharma), and the consequences of one's actions (karma). Its vivid imagery is best understood as moral allegory rather than literal cosmology — a teaching device meant to encourage compassion, honesty, and restraint in this life.

The Soul's Journey Through Yamaloka

Upon death, it is said the soul (jiva) leaves the body and is led by two fearsome messengers, the Yamadutas, on a long journey southward to Yamaloka, the realm of Yamaraja. The road is described as harsh and unlit, stretching across 86,000 yojanas, where the soul relives the weight of its own choices before ever reaching the hall of judgment.

Chitragupta, the Celestial Scribe

Seated beside Yamaraja is Chitragupta, keeper of the cosmic ledger, said to record every act, word, and intention of every living being across a lifetime. When the soul arrives, this ledger is read aloud — nothing omitted, nothing exaggerated — and it is against this record, not mere accusation, that judgment is passed.

The River Vaitarani

Between the world of the living and the halls of judgment flows the Vaitarani, a river described as thick with blood, bone, and grief — impassable to the guilty, who are pulled under by their own unresolved deeds, and crossable by the righteous, who are said to hold fast to the tail of a ritually gifted cow as they wade through.

Preta: The Waiting Soul

For a period traditionally described as twelve to thirteen days after death, the soul is said to linger as a preta, a formless, hungry, in-between being, dependent on the living performing shraddha rites (ritual offerings of food, water, and remembrance) to build it a symbolic new body strong enough to complete its journey. It is this belief that gives the Garuda Purana its central role in Hindu funeral tradition even today.

Punarjanma: The Wheel Turns Again

Punishment in the Narakas is described as neither eternal nor final. Once the weight of a particular sin has been "worked off," the text holds that the soul is released for punarjanma — rebirth — into a new body and circumstance shaped by whatever karma remains outstanding. Even hell, in this framework, is a corrective passage rather than a permanent sentence.

Structure of the Text

The Purana is broadly divided into the Purva Khanda (covering rituals, dharma, astrology, and worldly conduct) and the Uttara Khanda (covering the afterlife journey, judgment, and rebirth). This application draws dramatized, simplified illustrations from the Uttara Khanda's descriptions of the Narakas.

This app is a creative and educational interpretation, not a substitute for the original scripture or scholarly translation. Depictions of punishment are symbolic teaching devices from a centuries-old moral tradition, not literal predictions.

The Four Yugas

Hindu cosmology describes time as cyclical, unfolding across four great ages (Yugas) that together form one Mahayuga. Each age is said to see a gradual decline in dharma (righteousness), from an age of near-perfect virtue to one of moral struggle.

Satya Yuga (Krita Yuga)

The age of truth. Dharma stands on all four legs; humanity lives in virtue, wisdom, and near-perfect harmony. Traditionally said to last 1,728,000 years.

Treta Yuga

Dharma diminishes by a quarter. Virtue still predominates, but sacrifice, duty, and effort become necessary to maintain order. Traditionally said to last 1,296,000 years.

Dvapara Yuga

Dharma stands on two legs. Virtue and vice are balanced; disease, conflict, and moral ambiguity increase. Traditionally said to last 864,000 years.

Kali Yuga

The current age, in which dharma stands on a single leg. Marked by conflict, materialism, and moral decline, yet also offering the swiftest path to spiritual growth through sincere effort. Traditionally said to last 432,000 years.

We Are Living in Kali Yuga

Tradition holds that Kali Yuga began roughly 5,000 years ago, at the moment of Krishna's departure from the earthly realm, and that we remain near its very beginning — with many thousands of years still to unfold. It is described as an age where truth erodes quietly, where shortcuts are mistaken for wisdom, and where the line between virtue and self-interest blurs with each generation.

The Promise of Kalki

Several Puranas, including the Garuda Purana's broader tradition, foretell that at the darkest close of Kali Yuga, Vishnu will descend as Kalki, a warrior riding a white horse, to end corruption and restore dharma — after which the cycle begins again with a new Satya Yuga. The wheel of time, in this view, never truly ends; it only turns.

These durations are drawn from traditional cosmological texts and are presented here as cultural and philosophical context, not as scientific claims.
